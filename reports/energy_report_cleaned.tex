% --- SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION ---
\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

This report assesses the determinants of annual residential electricity consumption in Belgium using a cross-sectional sample of 2,500 households. Four research questions guide the analysis: (Q1) identifying the best predictive model; (Q2) quantifying the consumption difference between EPC label A and labels B--F; (Q3) assessing the direct effect of household income on consumption; and (Q4) testing whether the effect of the EPC label varies with household occupancy. 

Variables \texttt{roof\_color} and \texttt{pet\_ownership} were excluded \textit{a priori} as they represent neither functional nor socio-economic drivers of energy demand (Vasseur \& Kemp, 2015). This is confirmed by bivariate screenings, where consumption remains consistent across all categories.

% --- SECTION 2: DATA & PREPROCESSING ---
\section{Data and Preprocessing}\label{sec:data}

After removing group-specific rows (rows 1, 11, 40) and \textit{a priori} excluded variables, \texttt{epc\_label} (levels A--F; reference: A) and \texttt{building\_era} (Pre-1970, 1970--2000, Post-2000) were converted to factors. Fifteen incomplete rows ($<1\%$) were removed via listwise deletion, yielding $n = 2{,}482$.

\textbf{Outlier analysis.} A $3.0 \times \text{IQR}$ upper fence identified seven extreme values of \texttt{annual\_kwh} (Tukey, 1977). Five showed consumption in the millions of kWh --- physically impossible for residential households of their reported size, occupancy, and EPC class (Table~\ref{tab:outliers}) --- and were removed as confirmed data entry errors. Two high values (16,395 and 17,197 kWh) were retained as internally consistent: large homes ($268\text{--}304\,\text{m}^2$), seven occupants, Pre-1970, EPC E/F. The final analytical dataset contains \textbf{2,477 observations}.

% --- SECTION 3: STATISTICAL METHODS ---
\section{Statistical Methods}\label{sec:methods}

Forward variable selection was applied to the modeling dataset in the order specified in the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP): $\texttt{sq\_meters} \to \texttt{epc\_label} \to \texttt{occupancy\_count} \to \texttt{income\_euro} \to \texttt{epc\_label:occupancy\_count}$. A term was retained only if it was statistically significant ($p < 0.05$) \textbf{and} reduced the model AIC. After each step, residual diagnostics (residuals vs. fitted plots, Q-Q plots, Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, and GVIF) were performed. Where heteroscedasticity was detected, a log transformation of the outcome was applied and selection repeated (James et al., 2021).

To control the familywise error rate (FWER) across planned hypothesis tests (Q2, Q3, Q4) at $\alpha = 0.05$, the **Holm-Bonferroni stepwise procedure** was applied (Holm, 1979).

% --- SECTION 4: RESULTS ---
\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Model Selection and Diagnostics}\label{subsec:selection}

Forward selection on the original scale produced pronounced heteroscedasticity. A log transformation of \texttt{annual\_kwh} resolved both heteroscedasticity and residual non-normality ($p = 0.94$ on KS test).

The final model is:
$$\log(\widehat{\text{annual\_kwh}}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \text{sq\_meters} + \sum_{k \in \{B,\ldots,F\}} \beta_k \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{epc}=k] + \beta_7 \cdot \text{occupancy\_count}$$

with $R^2 = 0.891$, Adj. $R^2 = 0.891$, $\text{AIC} = -3349.3$, and $\text{RMSE} = 0.123$ (log scale). All GVIF values were below 2.4.

Relative to EPC A, consumption increases monotonically up to **+50.0\%** for EPC F.

\subsection{Research Questions}\label{subsec:rqs}

\textbf{Q1: Best predictive model.} Selected on parsimony grounds: $\log(\text{annual\_kwh}) \sim \text{sq\_meters} + \text{epc\_label} + \text{occupancy\_count}$ ($\text{AIC} = -3349.3$).

\textbf{Q2: EPC A vs. average B--F.} EPC A homes consume on average \textbf{21.6\% less} than the mean of B--F homes (ratio = 0.784, 95\% CI: 0.775--0.794; Holm-adjusted $p < 0.001$).

\textbf{Q3: Income effect.} The direct effect of income is negligible and non-significant: per €10,000 increase, ratio = 1.001 (95\% CI: 0.998--1.004; Holm-adjusted $p = 0.534$). Its effect is operating indirectly through living area ($r = 0.72$).

\textbf{Q4: EPC $\times$ occupancy interaction.} All interaction terms were non-significant ($p > 0.05$), confirming the EPC efficiency effect remains consistent regardless of household size.

% --- SECTION 5: ETHICS ---
\section{Ethical Considerations and Stakeholder Impact}\label{sec:ethics}

This project worked with sensitive household socio-economic indicators. Although anonymized, re-identification risks exist under multi-variable overlap, necessitating strict compliance with GDPR governance.

The results highlight issues in **energy equity**: lower-income households often face barriers to accessing energy-efficient housing. Policy instruments should focus on structural support (e.g., targeted renovation grants) rather than purely behavioral expectations. Furthermore, using such models in commercial algorithmic scoring (e.g., credit/insurance) would unfairly penalize low-income renters.

% --- SECTION 6: CONCLUSION ---
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Residential energy demand in Belgium is driven primarily by occupancy count, structural size, and EPC rating. The optimal log-linear model accounts for 89.1\% of variance while satisfying regression assumptions. EPC A buildings achieve a 21.6\% reduction relative to the B--F average. Household income exhibits no direct explanatory power once living space is controlled.
